*On the Difference Between Reduction and Simplicity*

When I flatten a board by hand, the first shavings are rarely the beautiful ones.

The plane touches only the high places. A corner yields a short curl. A ridge near the center produces another. Between them, the blade passes over wood without cutting anything at all.

The sound is uneven: a brief hiss, then silence, then another hiss.

As the high places disappear, the shavings grow longer. The plane begins to travel through the board rather than across a collection of isolated surfaces. Eventually, a single ribbon emerges from the mouth and runs nearly the full length of the sole.

It is thin enough for light to pass through. It coils beside the bench, holding the shape of something that no longer exists.

This is the point at which planing becomes most satisfying.

It is also the point at which it becomes dangerous.

The blade is sharp. The plane is tuned. The board is finally giving up those long, unbroken shavings that make the work feel effortless. The hand wants to take another pass simply because the last one felt so good.

But the plane does not know when the board is finished.

It only knows how to remove wood.

Reduction is an action. Simplicity is a condition.

The two often appear together, which is why they are so easily confused. A resolved object usually contains less than it might have. Unnecessary material, ornament, and explanation have been removed.

But removal alone does not produce simplicity.

A table can be stripped of its hardware, squared at every corner, and finished in a neutral color. Its joints can be hidden and its surface cleared of every visible interruption. The result may look minimal while remaining completely unresolved.

The proportions may still be timid. The top may be too heavy for the legs. The joinery may ignore the movement of the wood. Nothing appears excessive, yet nothing quite belongs.

The object has been reduced, but it has not become simple.

Simplicity does not begin with a preference for plain things. It is what remains when the internal arguments of an object have been settled.

This distinction matters because simplicity is often mistaken for a visual style. We associate it with bare rooms, pale walls, clean geometry, and objects that seem to contain as little information as possible.

But an object can be visually spare and still be confused. Another can contain considerable detail and remain simple, provided every detail participates in the same idea.

Minimalism describes an appearance. Simplicity describes a relationship.

It would be convenient to place the blame on tools—to cast the wide-belt sander as an instrument of brute force and the hand plane as an instrument of sensitivity. But tools do not carry that distinction within them.

A machine can remove exactly the material a structure no longer needs. A hand plane can ruin a board one delicate shaving at a time.

The difference lies in the question being asked before either tool touches the wood.

Are we removing material because it interferes with the joint, the proportion, or the use of the object? Or are we removing it because less has become an aesthetic habit?

Are we correcting the board, or merely making it thinner?

Essential subtraction answers a condition. Arbitrary reduction answers an image we have already decided to impose.

This is why the stopping point matters as much as the cut.

When a tabletop overhang is right, the space beneath it does not feel empty. The shadow clarifies where the top ends and the structure beneath it begins. Remove too little and the two forms crowd each other. Remove too much and they lose their relationship.

The gap is not successful because it is small. It is successful because it is exact.

The same thing happens in writing. A paragraph can lose half its words and become shorter without becoming clearer. Details disappear, but the confusion that required them remains.

A simpler sentence is not merely a reduced sentence. It is one in which the relationship between the remaining words has become unavoidable.

Fewer words may be the result. They cannot be the objective.

Back at the bench, the board eventually reaches a point where the winding sticks agree, the pencil marks have disappeared, and the straightedge no longer reveals a high place demanding correction.

The blade can still cut.

Another shaving would still rise from the mouth of the plane. It might be the most perfect one yet.

But it would no longer answer a problem in the board.

I set the plane on its side and leave the last shaving where it fell.

The next pass would not make the board simpler.

It would only make it thinner.
