Not long ago, I was in my workshop preparing to mill a board. As I inspected the timber, considering the cuts that would define its shape, I reflected on the core philosophy that had guided my hands for years. When I first began developing Latent Form Theory, I believed the central act of making was subtraction.
This seemed obvious. Woodworking itself is a subtractive process. A tree becomes a board. A board becomes a component. A component becomes an object. At every stage, something is removed. Material is cut away. Surfaces are refined. Excess is eliminated. The final form emerges not because something is endlessly added, but because everything that does not belong is gradually taken away. The maker reveals by removing.


For a time, I believed this was the foundation of the entire philosophy. The role of the craftsman was not to impose a vision upon the material, but to discover what was already present and remove whatever concealed it. I defined this as a distinction between alignment—listening to the material—and stylization—imposing a visual signature from the outside.

## Nothing reaches its highest expression by accumulation alone; it reaches it through the careful removal of everything that distracts from its essence.

## A Deeper Meaning Within Subtraction

But as I continued to examine this idea, a question began to trouble me.
What exactly was being removed?
At first, the answer seemed to be merely physical: the waste portions of the board, the unnecessary details of the design, the elements that distracted from clarity. But the deeper I explored the idea, the more I realized that material was only the physical evidence of a much deeper process.
The maker was not simply removing wood. The maker was removing possibilities.
Every piece of material contains an almost endless number of potential outcomes. A single board could become a table, a cabinet, a sculpture, a vessel, or something that has never been imagined before. Every cut, every decision, every measurement eliminates some futures while preserving others.
Creation is not the act of bringing something from nothing. It is the act of resolving something from everything.
This realization deepened my understanding of essential subtraction. It was not merely the removal of physical excess; it was the disciplined elimination of possibilities that moved away from the essential character of the work. Every choice closes a door. The form becomes clearer as competing possibilities disappear.

## An Incomplete Understanding

But eventually, another question emerged—one that challenged the foundation I had built.
If subtraction was the elimination of possibilities, was subtraction alone enough to explain creation?
I began to realize that it described only half of the process. Subtraction explains what is removed. It does not fully explain why what remains feels resolved.
Two objects can contain the same materials, the same dimensions, and the same number of elements, yet one feels inevitable while the other feels incomplete. Why?
An object can be resolved structurally and still fail compositionally. This failure does not look like excess; it looks like isolation. A single detail, well made and well placed by any individual measure, might have no partner elsewhere in the piece. It may exist correctly, yet still exist alone.
My earlier distinction between alignment and stylization had proven useful, but it was incomplete. A form can be reduced correctly by every material and structural measure and still feel unfinished, because reduction alone does not compose.

## The Discovery of Composition

This was the moment when the concept of composition revealed itself as a third mode.
Composition was not an additional step after subtraction. It was the intelligence within subtraction.
There is a relationship between maker and material that does not fit cleanly into alignment or stylization. It is not the material dictating a decision, nor is it the maker applying an inherited vocabulary. It is the maker exercising judgment about how the parts of an object address one another—placing weight, drawing the eye, creating tension and resolution, allowing one element to answer another.
Alignment asks: What does the material and structure require?
Stylization asks: What visual language do I want to impose?
Composition asks: How do these elements belong together as a whole?
While alignment listens to the material and stylization speaks through tradition, composition listens to the relationships within the object itself. It borrows nothing from the outside. It is the act of recognizing and shaping the internal conversation between elements.

## The Language of Rhyme

Composition most often becomes visible through rhyme.
Not rhyme in the poetic sense of repeated sound, but in the structural sense of one detail answering another across the distance of an object.
A band of end grain at the top of a form and a smaller echo of end grain lower down. A void repeated, or a void answered by a line. A subtle angle reflected somewhere else in the piece. These relationships create a sense of continuity that the eye recognizes even when the mind cannot immediately explain it.
A single unrepeated gesture tends to read as arbitrary because it has nothing to relate to. Two gestures in relationship begin to feel intentional. The eye is not identifying a rule; it is recognizing a conversation.
This is why some objects feel inevitable while others feel merely assembled. The difference is not always complexity, craftsmanship, or expense. Often, it is whether the elements are speaking to one another.

## The Complete Process of Latent Form Theory

Together, these elements form the complete process:
Alignment reveals possibility.
 The maker listens to what the material and structure require.
Subtraction eliminates distraction.
 The maker removes what obscures the inherent order.
Composition establishes harmony.
 The maker ensures that what remains participates in a larger relationship.
Latent Form Theory is not a philosophy of taking away. It is a philosophy of revealing through discernment.
Every creative act is a movement from infinite possibility toward singular expression. The maker’s responsibility is not to create more, but to discover what deserves to remain—and to ensure that every remaining element contributes to a larger order.
The highest expression of a work does not come from adding everything possible. It comes from recognizing what is essential, removing what is unnecessary, and allowing what remains to exist in complete relationship.
