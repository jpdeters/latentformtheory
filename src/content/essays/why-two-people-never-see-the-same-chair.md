---
title: "Why Two People Never See the Same Chair"
description: "Two people can look at the same chair and discover different things. An essay on disciplined attention, the choices held within an object, and what becomes visible when we look again."
date: "2026-10-21"
---

I stood before a handcrafted wooden chair beside another person, both of us looking directly at the same object. To my companion, it was simply a chair—a functional, everyday object serving a clear utility. But as I looked closer, I found myself reading a series of deliberate choices: a leg that could have flared outward for visual flourish but was kept strictly vertical, refusing decoration in favor of structural honesty; a mortise-and-tenon joint intentionally left exposed rather than hidden, admitting how the piece holds itself together instead of concealing the fact; a wood grain selected for its structural alignment rather than decorative appeal, so the material’s own logic carries the load rather than merely dressing it.

Both of us possessed healthy vision, yet we inhabited two completely different visual realities. One of us saw a category; the other saw a conversation of decisions.

The chair itself is incidental. The same distinction exists between the novice physician and the experienced diagnostician, the casual listener and the trained musician, the tourist and the architect. Expertise is rarely defined by possessing more information than someone else. More often, it is the refinement of perception—the gradual ability to recognize relationships, patterns, and intentions that were always present but previously invisible.

Perception is not a passive intake of light. The world does not suddenly become ordered when we learn to observe it; rather, our perception becomes disciplined enough to recognize the structural relationships and latent order that were already there. What we have never been trained to look for, we can look directly at and never truly see.

Perception, then, is not simply a biological function. It is a discipline of attention—a framework built through experience, comparison, correction, and intentional focus.

Looking identifies. Seeing interprets. Looking answers the question, *What is it?* Seeing asks, *Why is it this way?* The first is sufficient for navigating the world. The second opens the possibility of understanding it.

Attention is never neutral. Where I choose to direct my focus determines what becomes available to me. Nothing about the chair changes when I look at it this way—not one joint moves, not one fiber of grain shifts. The object remains exactly as it was. What changes is my ability to recognize its internal logic. The observer uncovers relationships that already exist.

That ability is cultivated like any other discipline through repeated, corrected encounters with both excellent and mediocre work until structural logic, proportion, and restraint become as immediately recognizable as color or shape. Experience teaches the eye to perceive what was always there.

Reflecting on this, I have come to realize that the true subject of Latent Form Theory was never merely furniture, architecture, or design. It was always the observer.

Before I can become a thoughtful maker—or a thoughtful judge of what others have made—I must remain willing to look beyond my first recognition. A hurried glance is often all that daily life allows. But it need not be the final encounter. We can return to an object, follow a line we passed over, notice the space between its parts, and discover a choice we had not yet learned to read.

The chair asks no more of us than it did before. Its joints hold. Its grain carries the load. The conversation remains there, available whenever we choose to look again.
